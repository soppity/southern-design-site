Water hammer and pulsation in pump systems: causes and fixes

Short answer

Water hammer is a pressure surge caused by liquid being stopped or started suddenly, usually by a fast-closing valve or a pump tripping. Pulsation is the rhythmic pressure swing that diaphragm, piston and other positive displacement pumps produce on every stroke. Both shake piping, break supports and damage gauges and seals. Both are fixed by slowing the change in velocity or absorbing it with a surge suppressor or pulsation dampener.

Why it hits so hard

Moving liquid has momentum. Stop it instantly in a steel pipe and the pressure rises by very roughly 50 psi for every 1 ft/s of velocity that was stopped. A line flowing at 8 ft/s can see a surge several times its working pressure.

Fixing water hammer

  • Slow the valve. Longer closing times and controlled actuator speed remove most surges. Positioners give that control on automated valves.
  • Use non-slam check valves on pump discharges.
  • Ramp pumps down with a variable frequency drive instead of tripping them.
  • Use pump control and surge anticipating valves on long pipelines.
  • Fit a surge suppressor close to the valve or pump that causes the surge.

Fixing pulsation

  • Fit a pulsation dampener on the discharge, as close to the pump as practical.
  • Set the dampener charge to the manufacturer's recommendation for your system pressure. A dampener with the wrong charge does very little.
  • On the suction side, an inlet stabilizer keeps the pump fed and stops the suction line shaking.
  • Use flexible connectors to keep vibration out of rigid pipework.

When to model it

On long lines, high velocities or systems with several pumps and valves, surge behavior is hard to judge by eye. Hydraulic simulation shows where the pressure peaks will be and what size of suppressor or change in valve timing removes them.

Frequently asked questions

Is water hammer dangerous?

It can be. Repeated surges loosen supports and fatigue joints, and a severe surge can exceed the pressure rating of pipe, valves and instruments.

Where should a pulsation dampener be installed?

On the pump discharge, as close to the pump as practical, with as few bends as possible between the two.

Do I need a dampener on the suction side too?

If the suction line is long or the pump is starving on each stroke, an inlet stabilizer helps the pump fill and protects the suction piping.
