Is your pump running off its curve? How to tell and what to do

Short answer

A centrifugal pump is designed to run near its best efficiency point (BEP). Run it well to the left or right of that point and the hydraulic forces on the impeller rise sharply, bending the shaft and overloading the seal and bearings. If the same pump keeps needing seals or bearings, find out where it actually operates before replacing parts again.

Left or right of best efficiency point

Where it runsWhat you noticeUsual reasons
Left of BEP (too little flow)High discharge pressure, low motor amps, heat, vibration, noise that eases when the discharge valve opensOversized pump, throttled discharge valve, demand lower than design
Right of BEP (too much flow)Low discharge pressure, high amps or motor trips, cavitation noiseSystem resistance lower than design, bypass left open, a parallel pump switched off

How to find the operating point

  1. Read suction and discharge pressure at the pump.
  2. Convert the difference to head: feet of head = psi x 2.31 / specific gravity.
  3. Read flow if there is a meter, and motor amps.
  4. Plot the point on the manufacturer's curve for the impeller diameter and speed actually installed.

If the point sits well away from BEP, or does not sit on the curve at all, you have found something worth fixing. A point below the curve means internal wear or a wrong impeller.

How far off is too far

The Hydraulic Institute describes a preferred operating region, commonly quoted as roughly 70% to 120% of BEP flow for most centrifugal pumps. Inside it, life is governed by normal wear. Outside it, shaft deflection, recirculation and cavitation shorten the life of seals and bearings.

Why it happens

Safety factors get stacked at the design stage, so the pump arrives oversized. Processes change and nobody revisits the pump. Or a control valve is left to absorb the difference, burning energy and pushing the pump left.

Ways to fix it

  • Trim the impeller to suit the real duty.
  • Fit a variable frequency drive and slow the pump down.
  • Replace the pump or the impeller with one sized for the actual flow and head.
  • Add a minimum-flow bypass for pumps that must run at low demand.
  • Remove the restriction in the system if the pump is being held back.

Southern Design models the system with computer hydraulic simulation to choose between these before money is spent, and supplies Toshiba drives where slowing the pump is the answer.

Protect the pump in the meantime

A power monitor reads motor load and trips the pump when it drifts too far in either direction. It is inexpensive protection while the permanent fix is planned.

Frequently asked questions

What is best efficiency point?

It is the flow at which the pump converts the most shaft power into hydraulic power. It is also where the forces on the impeller are most balanced, which is why reliability is best there.

Can I leave the discharge valve throttled permanently?

It works hydraulically, but it wastes energy at the valve, and if it pushes the pump too far left it trades one problem for another. Treat it as a temporary measure.

How do I convert pressure to head?

Feet of head equals pressure in psi multiplied by 2.31 and divided by the liquid's specific gravity. For water, 10 psi is about 23 feet.
