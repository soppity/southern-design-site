Why does my mechanical seal keep failing?

Short answer

A mechanical seal that fails repeatedly is almost always reacting to something else in the pump or the system: dry running, a flush that is not reaching the faces, shaft movement from running away from the pump's best efficiency point, or materials that do not suit the fluid. Replacing the seal treats the symptom. Reading the failed seal faces tells you which cause to fix.

Dry running and lost flush

Seal faces ride on a film of liquid a few microns thick. Lose that film for even a short time and the faces overheat. It happens when a pump is started before it is primed and vented, when the flush line or its orifice is blocked, or when the suction tank runs empty.

  • Vent the seal chamber before every start.
  • Confirm flush flow at the seal, not only at the source.
  • Fit a power monitor that trips the motor on low load, the signature of a dry pump.
  • On water-flushed seals, use seal water control so flow is set and visible.

Running away from best efficiency point

A centrifugal pump run well to the left or right of its best efficiency point puts an uneven load on the impeller. The shaft bends slightly on every revolution, the seal faces open and close, and they wear or chip. If the pump also eats bearings, this is the first place to look. See how to tell if a pump is running off its curve.

Wrong materials for the fluid

Face pairs and elastomers have to suit the liquid, its temperature and any solids. A carbon face that works in clean water will not last in a slurry, and an elastomer that suits one chemical can swell or dissolve in another. Process changes, new cleaning chemicals and higher temperatures all catch out seals that were correctly chosen years ago.

A flush plan that no longer fits

The flush plan controls the environment at the faces. Recirculating process liquid from the discharge works for clean, cool fluids. Liquids that are hot, dirty or close to their boiling point usually need a clean external flush or a dual seal with a barrier fluid. If the service has changed since the pump was installed, review the plan.

Installation and mechanical condition

A seal cannot run true on a shaft that does not. Check shaft runout, end play and bearing condition before fitting a new seal; a commonly quoted limit for runout at the seal is about 0.002 in (0.05 mm). Component seals are set by hand and are easy to set wrong. Cartridge seals arrive preset and remove that risk.

How to stop the repeat

  1. Keep the failed seal. Do not clean it.
  2. Photograph both faces and the O-rings.
  3. Record run hours since the last change, and the flow, pressures and temperature the pump was running at.
  4. Have the seal examined and the cause written down, not just the part replaced.
  5. Track failures per pump so the next one is compared with the last.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a mechanical seal last?

There is no single figure. In a clean, cool, steady service a seal commonly runs for several years. If the same pump needs a seal more than once a year, the cause is in the pump or the system, not the seal.

Is some leakage from a mechanical seal normal?

A working seal passes a very small amount of vapor that you normally cannot see. A visible, steady drip means the faces are damaged or not closing.

Will a cartridge seal fix repeat failures?

It removes installation and setting errors. It will not fix dry running, a failed flush or a pump operating off its curve.

Can a failed mechanical seal be repaired?

Often, yes. Faces can be relapped or replaced and elastomers renewed, and an exchange program keeps a ready seal on the shelf while the failed one is rebuilt.
